Governance + Community of Practice: Two Jobs, Both Needed

Governance decides what must be true. A Community of Practice gets people good at making it true. Most organizations have one and wonder why the other problem never goes away.
Rules. Craft. Standards.

Rules without craft become paperwork. Craft without rules becomes everyone doing it their own way. You need a group that can say no, and a group where people learn to do the work well. They are not the same group, and merging them breaks both.

Two jobs, side by side

Governance (the council)Community of Practice
PurposeDecisions, accountability, controlsSkill, shared know-how, consistency
MembershipNamed roles — for data work: compliance, data, operations, a business voiceOpen to anyone who builds or uses the work
AttendanceRequiredVoluntary
OutputApprovals, retirements, the register, policyTemplates, patterns, how-tos, lessons learned
AuthorityCan say noCan recommend, never mandate
Measured byControls met, risk downPatterns reused, time to deliver the governed way
RhythmMonthly, agenda-drivenEvery two to four weeks, demos and show-and-tell

The feedback loop

GUARDRAILS→PRACTICE→PATTERN→PROPOSE→STANDARD

Governance sets the guardrail. The community works out how to meet it well. When an approach proves itself, the community proposes it, and governance makes it the standard. Here is how that plays out on an acquisition:

  1. The council sets the ruleFor example: every report must reconcile to the one it replaces before cutover.
  2. The community shares howAn analyst walks through the reconciliation query that worked on the last acquired firm’s data.
  3. The pattern proves itselfThe next team uses it on the next deal, and it works again.
  4. The community proposes itAs a recommendation — the community never mandates.
  5. The council adopts itNow it is the standard, and the next acquisition starts from it instead of from scratch.
“Governance tells an acquired team the rules. The community is where they actually join.”
Acquired teams join through the community, not the rulebook.

How each one starts

Standing up the council

  1. Find the sponsor — one senior leader who owns the outcome and backs the council’s decisions.
  2. Write a one-page charter — purpose, decision rights, members, cadence, how decisions are recorded.
  3. Name the seats, not just the people — each seat has a holder and an alternate.
  4. Seed the register first — the first agenda works from real items, not slides.
  5. Meet three times, close together — every two weeks, to show the council actually decides. Then monthly.
  6. Keep a decision log — every approval, rejection and retirement, dated, with the reason.

Starting the Community of Practice

  1. Name a coordinator and a small core team — three to five practitioners, not managers.
  2. Start from a real pain — the first session solves something people are stuck on. No launch event.
  3. Invite widely, require nothing — including the people from every acquired team, from day one.
  4. Give it a home — one channel and one shared page, so knowledge outlives the meeting.
  5. Set the rhythm — a short demo, a real problem worked together, one pattern written up.
  6. Connect it to the council — the coordinator carries proven patterns up and new rules back.

How they keep going

The council

Charter reviewed every quarter. The business seat rotates every six months, so more of the field has a voice. Each new acquisition is a standing agenda item until its legacy work is retired. Results go to the sponsor monthly.

The Community of Practice

Facilitation rotates, so it never depends on one person. Each acquired team gets a welcome session and a buddy. Patterns that work twice are proposed as standards. It watches simple signs of life: attendance, patterns reused, and how fast new members deliver their first piece the governed way.

Two ways it fails

  • The community starts mandating. The moment it says “you must,” people stop bringing it their problems. Mandates belong to governance.
  • The council only talks. If it discusses but never decides, the register goes stale and the rules stop meaning anything.
