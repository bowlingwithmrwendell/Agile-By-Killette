Trust & Protection: A New Scrum Master's First Days

Your first job as a new Scrum Master isn't the board. It's their trust. And the fastest way to earn it is to protect them — visibly.
From ceremony to ownership.

Gaining a new team's trust is one of my top priorities the moment I join. Not the board, not the burndown — the trust. Twenty years of mostly-remote assignments taught me it's earned in the first few calls, in small, deliberate moves. Here they are.

Earning trust, from the first call

  1. Be genuinely welcoming.When you facilitate that first call, acknowledge each person by name as they join. It costs ten seconds and it tells everyone the room is theirs too.
  2. Promise total transparency — and leave no doubt.Tell the team you will always be straight with them. Then be. The promise only counts the first time you keep it under pressure.
  3. Name your job as protector.Say out loud that one of your roles is to protect the team — and commit to it. People need to know the shield exists before they'll trust it.
  4. Listen, observe, collaborate — mostly listen.Observe before you advise. The answers you'd hand them are worth less than the ones they find with you.
  5. Everyone's concerns are important.Not just the loudest voice, and not just the senior one. The quiet objection is usually the expensive one.
  6. Don't talk over anyone.Space is respect. Interrupting a person once teaches the whole team what your listening is worth.
  7. Be timely when you owe them an answer.You're normally working inside a two-week time box. A slow reply doesn't just annoy people — it costs real ground in a sprint that can't get it back.
  8. Genuinely express your care and interest."How are you feeling this morning?" … "Sounds like that cold is finally letting up." … "How's the baby doing — she's got to be about six months now, growing like a weed I bet." Remember the details. The details say I see you.
  9. Have fun and be interesting.Nobody trusts the person who makes the meeting heavier. Bring some life with you.
  10. Let your soft skills shine as much as the technical ones.Process expertise gets you in the room. The human skills are what make the team let you coach them.
"Do all this, and here's what you hear at the end of the assignment: 'I'll miss you. Your kind thoughts, your motivation, your friendship.'"
— That's how you know it worked.

When a team member is struggling

We talk about it — never in shame, always "how can I help?" But here's what "how can I help" really means: how do I get you what you need?

Protecting the team isn't shielding it from accountability. It's making sure every member has what they need to carry their part. You remove the obstacle, not the person. Nobody gets blamed, nobody gets left behind — and that's exactly how the team keeps its ownership, together.

The quiet cost of ignoring it: a struggling member left to sink quietly is a team already losing ownership. A member who's supported stays in the boat, rowing.

Why protection is the shortcut to trust

Protection isn't a separate duty from trust — it's one of the fastest ways a team learns to trust you. When people see you take the hits — block side-door work, absorb executive pressure, refuse to let anyone get blamed or left behind — they stop bracing and start trusting. The shield earns the trust.

Where this comes from

This isn't theory. It's what I've practiced across 20+ years of mostly-remote assignments — and what I teach pro bono, mentoring new Scrum Masters as a volunteer. Coaching for pay, coaching bowlers for fun, coaching new Scrum Masters for free: same wiring, three lanes. The through-line since I was thirteen — to help.

Why it matters beyond the first week: a team that trusts you will let you coach them. A team that doesn't will only comply with you. Trust is the precondition for ownership — everything in the Agile Lanes sits on top of it.
